\subsection{Two-dimensional \texttt{BarDistribution} head}
\label{sec:2d-bardistribution}

Training is asking how plausible each location is at every query. Then looking up how tall the predicted surface is there, and penalising when it's short.

\paragraph{What the head predicts.}
For every query covariate $X$, given the observational context
$\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{obs}} = \{(X_i, T_i, Y_i)\}_{i=1}^{N}$, the head
outputs a full probability density over the pair of potential outcomes
under both treatment values:
\begin{equation}
  f(y_0, y_1 \mid X, \mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{obs}})
  \;:\; \mathbb{R}^2 \to [0, \infty),
  \qquad
  \iint_{\mathbb{R}^2}
    f(y_0, y_1 \mid X, \mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{obs}})\, dy_0\, dy_1 = 1.
\end{equation}
Every point in $\mathbb{R}^2$ has a defined density value; nothing is
excluded. This is an extension of the one-dimensional head, which outputs
$f(y \mid X, \mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{obs}})$ over a single outcome
$y \in \mathbb{R}$.

\paragraph{Why the joint, not two marginals.}
A pair of marginal densities
$\hat p_0(y_0 \mid X, \mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{obs}})$ and
$\hat p_1(y_1 \mid X, \mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{obs}})$
recovers the true joint only if $Y_{\mathrm{do}(0)} \perp Y_{\mathrm{do}(1)} \mid X$
--- an assumption that fails on essentially every real DGP, where the
potential outcomes share exogenous noise.

\paragraph{Preprocessing: rescaling outcomes to $[-1, 1]$.}
Before the head is applied, every $Y_i$ in the context is min--max
rescaled by the context $Y$-range so it lies in $[-1, 1]$:
\begin{equation}
  \tilde{Y}_i \;=\; 2 \cdot \frac{Y_i - Y_{\min}}{Y_{\max} - Y_{\min}} - 1,
  \qquad
  Y_{\min} = \min_i Y_i,\; Y_{\max} = \max_i Y_i.
  \label{eq:minmax}
\end{equation}
This is standard input normalisation so a single interior grid of fixed width is meaningful across queries, and so the tail scales
$\sigma_d^{(\ell)}$ below are comparable across queries. The choice of
$[-1, 1]$ (as opposed to, say, $[0, 1]$) is a convention. The scale is inverted at test time to report outcomes in the
original units.

For the rest of this subsection $y_0, y_1$ refer to the rescaled scale,
so context observations lie in $[-1, 1]$.

\paragraph{Why $f$ must cover all of $\mathbb{R}^2$, not just $[-1, 1]^2$.}
At training time we cannot guarantee that a labelled paired outcome
$(y_0^\star, y_1^\star)$ from the DGP will fall inside the rescaling
box $[-1, 1]^2$ --- rescaling in \eqref{eq:minmax} is done on the
\emph{context} $Y$-values, and the target held-out outcome can easily
land outside the observed context range. If $f$ assigns zero density
to any labelled target, the training loss
$-\log f(y_0^\star, y_1^\star \mid X, \mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{obs}})$
diverges to $+\infty$ and the model becomes untrainable. So $f$ must
place strictly positive density everywhere in $\mathbb{R}^2$. This is
the constraint that forces the piecewise construction below. However, we would still expect most of the training prediction to fall under the $[-1, 1]^2$ range.

\paragraph{Three-part construction.}
A plain discrete grid over $[-1,1]^2$, with one probability per bin, is
flexible enough to capture the complexity of our datasets, but it cannot be
extended to all of $\mathbb{R}^2$ without requiring infinitely many
parameters. Restricting the grid to a bounded region keeps the parameter
count finite, but it also forces the predicted density to vanish outside the
box. What we need, then, is a component that assigns non-zero density over
the whole of $\mathbb{R}^2$ while still being described by finitely many
parameters --- a Gaussian being the natural candidate. On its own, however, a
Gaussian is too rigid to fit multi-modal or skewed joints. We therefore combine the two, which partitions $\mathbb{R}^2$ into three
kinds of region. Each tail is a half-normal anchored at the boundary: a normal density centred at $+1$ or $-1$, restricted to the side lying outside $\mathcal{I}$ and renormalised. Anchoring at the boundary removes
the location parameter $(\mu)$, leaving the scale $\sigma$ as the only free quantity, and $\sigma$ admits a direct reading --- it tells you how far your model should predict fall past the boundary. If true values leave $[-1,1]$ so only marginally, as with $1.02$ or $-1.05$, the
likelihood favours a small $\sigma$; if they routinely land far outside,
a larger $\sigma$ is favoured. Learning $\sigma$ is therefore  the act of learning how far the true distribution extends beyond the modelled
region, and it assigns a corresponding amount of probability mass there. We prefer to use a Gaussian tail for three reasons: (1) it is normalisable on the half-line with a closed-form constant, (2) it has a closed-form CDF; (3) its log-density is quadratic, so gradients with respect to both the observation and $\sigma$ are smooth throughout the domain. Heavier-tailed alternatives such as a truncated Student-$t$ are equally tractable but introduce a further shape parameter that is weakly identified when few observations fall outside $\mathcal{I}$; the Gaussian tail proved sufficient on our
datasets. 

The following provides the three parts:
 
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \emph{Interior} $\mathcal{I} \coloneqq [-1, 1]^2$: a
    $K \times K$ grid of bins, with one learned probability per bin.
    Gives a piecewise-constant density that can approximate any joint
    shape in the bulk.
  \item \emph{Edge} (one coordinate outside $\mathcal{I}$, one inside):
    a half-Gaussian tail along the outside coordinate, multiplied by
    the grid's boundary conditional along the inside coordinate. Four
    edges (one per axis-direction pair) continuously extend the grid
    past $\mathcal{I}$ in each single direction.
  \item \emph{Corner} (both coordinates outside $\mathcal{I}$): a
    bivariate normal truncated to the appropriate quadrant, with the
    correlation inherited from the interior grid. Four corners extend
    the density into the four diagonal directions.
\end{enumerate}
These nine regions (one interior, four edge, four corner) partition
$\mathbb{R}^2$. The head's density is a weighted mixture over the nine,
which is strictly positive everywhere by construction.

\paragraph{What the head learns.}
For each query the transformer emits a fixed-size vector of
$K^2 + 9 + 4$ real numbers: $K^2$ interior-bin logits, $9$ region-weight
logits, and $4$ raw tail scales (one per axis-direction pair). Standard
link functions (softmax on the first two groups, softplus on the third,
detailed below) turn these into the interior probabilities
$p_{j_0, j_1}$, the region weights $w_r$, and the tail scales
$\sigma_d^{(\ell)}$ that together determine the density $f$. Training
is a single objective: for each labelled paired outcome
$(y_0^\star, y_1^\star)$, minimise the negative log-density
$-\log f(y_0^\star, y_1^\star \mid X, \mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{obs}})$.
Gradient descent flows through the softmax and softplus link functions
back into the transformer's parameters, which learn to push more
density toward observed outcomes at each step. No auxiliary losses.

The rest of this subsection formalises each piece: the interior bin
structure, the per-query output that parameterises everything, the
density expression in each of the three region types, how the tail
correlation is read from the interior grid rather than trained
separately, and finally the unified mixture density and loss.

\paragraph{Interior bin structure.}
$\mathcal{I} = [-1, 1]^2$ is partitioned into a uniform $K \times K$
grid of square bins of width $\delta \coloneqq 2/K$. Bins are indexed
by $(j_0, j_1) \in \{0, 1, \ldots, K-1\}^2$; bin $B_{j_0, j_1}$ is the
$\delta \times \delta$ square with lower-left corner
$(-1 + j_0 \delta,\; -1 + j_1 \delta)$. Under this convention a
rescaled coordinate $y \in [-1, 1]$ belongs to bin index
\begin{equation}
  j_\ell(y) \;\coloneqq\; \lfloor (y + 1)/\delta \rfloor,
  \label{eq:bin-index}
\end{equation}
which appears throughout the density expressions below.

\paragraph{Per-query output.}
For each query $X$, the transformer emits $K^2 + 9 + 4$ real numbers,
grouped into three sets. Each set is transformed into a valid density
parameter with a standard link function that guarantees positivity:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Interior logits} $\ell \in \mathbb{R}^{K^2}$, one per
    bin, softmaxed into a discrete joint on $\mathcal{I}$:
    \begin{equation}
      p_{j_0, j_1}(X, \mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{obs}})
      \;=\;
      \frac{\exp(\ell_{j_0, j_1})}
           {\sum_{i_0, i_1} \exp(\ell_{i_0, i_1})},
      \qquad
      \sum_{j_0, j_1} p_{j_0, j_1} = 1.
      \label{eq:pmat-softmax}
    \end{equation}
    The softmax gives every bin strictly positive probability, so no
    bin can zero out the density on any target.
  \item \emph{Region logits} $u \in \mathbb{R}^{9}$, one per region,
    softmaxed into the nine mixture weights:
    \begin{equation}
      w_r(X, \mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{obs}})
      \;=\; \frac{\exp(u_r)}{\sum_{s=1}^{9} \exp(u_s)},
      \qquad r \in \{1, \dots, 9\},\ \sum_r w_r = 1.
      \label{eq:region-weights}
    \end{equation}
    These control how much mass the model allocates to the interior
    vs.\ the four edges vs.\ the four corners.
  \item \emph{Raw tail parameters}
    $(\sigma_{L}^{(0)\text{raw}}, \sigma_{R}^{(0)\text{raw}},
      \sigma_{L}^{(1)\text{raw}}, \sigma_{R}^{(1)\text{raw}})$,
    one per axis $\ell \in \{0, 1\}$ and direction $d \in \{L, R\}$,
    passed through softplus with a small floor $\varepsilon > 0$:
    \begin{equation}
      \sigma_{d}^{(\ell)}(X, \mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{obs}})
      \;=\; \delta \cdot
      \bigl(\mathrm{softplus}\bigl(\sigma_{d}^{(\ell)\text{raw}}\bigr)
            + \varepsilon\bigr).
      \label{eq:tail-scales}
    \end{equation}
    Softplus guarantees $\sigma > 0$ so the tail Gaussians below are
    well-defined; the $\delta$ multiplier makes tail widths and
    interior bin widths comparable so the interior--tail seam is
    smooth in scale.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{The full density, one region at a time.}
The density $f(y_0, y_1 \mid X, \mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{obs}})$ is defined
piecewise depending on where $(y_0, y_1)$ falls relative to the box
$\mathcal{I}$. Below we describe each of the three cases; the full
mixture is stated in
\eqref{eq:full-density}.

\subparagraph{Case 1 --- interior ($y_0, y_1 \in [-1, 1]$).}
The discrete grid \eqref{eq:pmat-softmax} defines a piecewise-constant
density on $\mathcal{I}$: mass $p_{j_0, j_1}$ is spread uniformly over
the area $\delta^2$ of bin $B_{j_0, j_1}$, giving density
$p_{j_0, j_1}/\delta^2$ inside that bin:
\begin{equation}
  f_{\mathrm{inner}}(y_0, y_1 \mid X, \mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{obs}})
  \;=\;
  \frac{p_{j_0(y_0),\, j_1(y_1)}}{\delta^{2}}.
  \label{eq:inner-density}
\end{equation}
Both the bin assignment $j_\ell(\cdot)$ in \eqref{eq:bin-index} and the
softmax weights in \eqref{eq:pmat-softmax} are differentiable in the
logits $\ell$, so gradients flow back through this density to the
transformer.

\subparagraph{Case 2 --- edge (one coordinate outside).}
When exactly one of $y_0, y_1$ falls outside $[-1, 1]$, we need a
density that (i) smoothly extends off the boundary in the outside
coordinate, and (ii) still reflects where along the boundary the point
sits in the inside coordinate. Take the representative subcase
$y_0 \in [-1, 1]$ and $y_1 < -1$ (the other three edges are
symmetric). We factor
\begin{equation}
  f_{\mathrm{mix}}(y_0, y_1 \mid X, \mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{obs}})
  \;=\;
  p_{L}^{(1)}(y_1)\, \cdot\, g(y_0 \mid y_1 = -1),
  \label{eq:mix-density}
\end{equation}
where the tail contribution
\begin{equation}
  p_{L}^{(1)}(y_1)
  \;=\;
  \frac{2}{\sigma_{L}^{(1)}}\,
  \phi\!\left(\tfrac{y_1 - (-1)}{\sigma_{L}^{(1)}}\right)
\end{equation}
is a half-Gaussian on $(-\infty, -1]$ centred at the boundary $-1$
with scale $\sigma_L^{(1)}$ ($\phi$ is the standard normal density,
the factor $2$ makes the half-normal integrate to one on the half-line),
and
\begin{equation}
  g(y_0 \mid y_1 = -1)
  \;=\;
  \frac{p_{j_0(y_0),\, 0}}
       {\bigl(\sum_{i_0} p_{i_0,\, 0}\bigr) \cdot \delta}
  \label{eq:boundary-conditional}
\end{equation}
is the discrete boundary conditional obtained by evaluating $p$ on the
bottom row (bins with $j_1 = 0$, i.e., adjacent to $y_1 = -1$) and
renormalising over the in-range axis so the density integrates to one
along $y_0$. The approximation
$p(y_0 \mid y_1 < -1) \approx p(y_0 \mid y_1 = -1)$
is discussed in \S\ref{sec:2d-bardistribution:notes} below. All four
edge subcases apply the same construction with the boundary and axis
adjusted accordingly.

\subparagraph{Case 3 --- corner (both coordinates outside).}
When both $y_0$ and $y_1$ fall outside $[-1, 1]$, the point lies in
one of four diagonal quadrants determined by signs
$(s_0, s_1) \in \{L, R\}^{2}$. We use a truncated bivariate normal on
that quadrant, centred at the corresponding corner
$(c_0, c_1) \in \{-1, +1\}^{2}$, scaled by the tail widths
$(\sigma_{s_0}^{(0)}, \sigma_{s_1}^{(1)})$ from \eqref{eq:tail-scales}:
\begin{equation}
  f_{\mathrm{tail}}(y_0, y_1 \mid X, \mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{obs}})
  \;=\;
  \frac{1}{Z(s_0, s_1, \rho)}\,
  \phi_{2}\!\left(\tilde{y}_0, \tilde{y}_1;\, \rho\right),
  \qquad
  \tilde{y}_\ell = \frac{y_\ell - c_\ell}{\sigma_{s_\ell}^{(\ell)}},
  \label{eq:tail-density}
\end{equation}
where $\phi_{2}(\cdot, \cdot; \rho)$ is the standard bivariate normal
density with correlation $\rho$, and the denominator $Z$ is the total
probability mass the bivariate normal assigns to that quadrant --- the
correct normaliser to make $f_{\mathrm{tail}}$ integrate to one on the
quadrant. This mass is given in closed form by Sheppard's identity
\citep{sheppard1899application}:
\begin{equation}
  Z(s_0, s_1, \rho)
  \;=\;
  \begin{cases}
    \tfrac{1}{4} + \tfrac{\arcsin\rho}{2\pi}
      & \text{if } s_0 = s_1, \\[4pt]
    \tfrac{1}{4} - \tfrac{\arcsin\rho}{2\pi}
      & \text{if } s_0 \ne s_1.
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:sheppard}
\end{equation}

To demonstrate this normalization step by $Z$, consider the top-right corner, that is the region
where $y_0 > 1$ and $y_1 > 1$, on which a bivariate normal centred at $(1,1)$ is placed. A bivariate normal is supported on the entire plane, so centring it at $(1,1)$ also assigns density to points such as $(0.9, 1.2)$, $(1.3, 0.8)$ and $(0.5, 0.4)$, none of which lie in the corner region: they belong to the top edge, the right edge and the
interior respectively, each of which is already modelled by its own component. Left uncorrected, the corner component would therefore claim mass that has already been accounted for elsewhere, and the nine
regional densities would not combine into a single normalised density on
$\mathbb{R}^2$. 

The correlation $\rho$ used here is not a separate learned parameter;
it is derived directly from the interior grid, as described next.

\paragraph{Correlation of the tails inherited from the interior grid.}
The correlation $\rho$ in
\eqref{eq:tail-density}--\eqref{eq:sheppard} is defined as the Pearson
correlation of the discrete distribution $\{p_{j_0, j_1}\}$ evaluated at
bin midpoints $m_\ell(j_\ell) \coloneqq -1 + \delta(j_\ell + \tfrac{1}{2})$:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\bar{y}_\ell \;&=\; \sum_{j_0, j_1} p_{j_0, j_1}\, m_\ell(j_\ell), \\
v_\ell \;&=\; \sum_{j_0, j_1} p_{j_0, j_1}
             \bigl(m_\ell(j_\ell) - \bar{y}_\ell\bigr)^{2}, \\
c \;&=\; \sum_{j_0, j_1} p_{j_0, j_1}
        \bigl(m_0(j_0) - \bar{y}_0\bigr)
        \bigl(m_1(j_1) - \bar{y}_1\bigr), \\
\rho \;&=\; \frac{c}{\sqrt{v_0\, v_1} + \varepsilon}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:rho-derived}
\end{equation}
Two reasons for deriving $\rho$ this way rather than training a
separate scalar. First, it removes one degree of freedom, so the model
cannot learn a $\rho$ for the tails that disagrees with what the
interior grid already implies. Second, whatever coupling
$(Y_0, Y_1)$ shows in the interior bulk is exactly what governs the
tails, so the density is coherent across the seam between $\mathcal{I}$
and its complement.

\paragraph{Full mixture and training loss.}
The joint density at any point in $\mathbb{R}^2$ is the mixture of the
nine region components weighted by
\eqref{eq:region-weights}:
\begin{equation}
  f(y_0, y_1 \mid X, \mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{obs}})
  \;=\;
  w_{1}\, f_{\mathrm{inner}}
  \;+\!\sum_{r = 2}^{5}\! w_{r}\, f^{(r)}_{\mathrm{mix}}
  \;+\!\sum_{r = 6}^{9}\! w_{r}\, f^{(r)}_{\mathrm{tail}},
  \label{eq:full-density}
\end{equation}
where the four $f^{(r)}_{\mathrm{mix}}$ correspond to the four edge
orientations and the four $f^{(r)}_{\mathrm{tail}}$ to the four
corners. Because the nine regions partition $\mathbb{R}^2$, at any
query point exactly one region is active and only its component
contributes to $f$.

Given a labelled paired outcome $(y_0^\star, y_1^\star)$, the training
loss is the negative log-density
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}(y_0^\star, y_1^\star \mid X, \mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{obs}})
  \;=\;
  -\log\, f(y_0^\star, y_1^\star \mid X, \mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{obs}}),
  \label{eq:nll}
\end{equation}
averaged over queries in a batch. Standard backpropagation flows
gradients through the softmax and softplus link functions back into
the transformer weights, which learn to push more density towards the
observed $(y_0^\star, y_1^\star)$ at each step. There is no auxiliary
loss.

\paragraph{Notes on the edge-case approximation.}
\label{sec:2d-bardistribution:notes}
The edge density \eqref{eq:mix-density} factors as
$p(y_1)\,p(y_0 \mid y_1)$ and supplies the second factor by the boundary
conditional $p(y_0 \mid y_1 = -1)$, holding it fixed for all
$y_1 < -1$. The approximation is exact when $Y_0 \perp Y_1$, since the
conditional is then constant in $y_1$. Under dependence it displaces the
conditional by an amount that grows with both the strength of dependence
and the distance past the boundary: for a bivariate normal with
correlation $\rho$ and tail scale $\sigma$, the resulting excess
negative log-likelihood per edge observation is approximately
$\rho^{2}\sigma^{2} / \pi(1 - \rho^{2})$.

This displacement is not something the edges could avoid by inheriting
$\rho$ in the way the corner components do. In a corner both
coordinates are Gaussian, so dependence is carried by a single scalar
and the conditional mean shifts automatically with distance from the
corner. An edge instead couples a Gaussian tail to a histogram, for
which no such scalar exists: letting the boundary conditional drift
would require an explicit model of how the bin probabilities deform
with $y_1$, whose parameters would have to be estimated from the few
observations falling outside $\mathcal{I}$. We therefore accept a
bounded bias in place of that variance, and retain a density that is
differentiable and evaluable in $\mathcal{O}(1)$ per query.

\subsection{Multimodal density recovery via mixture MALC}
\label{sec:malc-multimodal}

The training-time head outputs the discrete grid
$\{p_{j_0, j_1}\}$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:pmat-softmax}, which is a
piecewise-constant density on $\mathcal{I}$. To recover a smooth
density we build on the mean-adjusted log-concave (MALC) estimator of
\citet{danisman2026malc}: MALC produces a shape-constrained
nonparametric density estimate from grouped (binned) probabilities
without bandwidth selection. Because a single log-concave density is
unimodal by construction, we extend MALC to a $K$-component mixture in
order to represent the multimodal joints that arise in causal
inference under posterior uncertainty over compatible SCMs.  We refer to \citet{danisman2026malc} for
the theoretical properties of the estimator and its behaviour under
grid refinement. We denote a single MALC component
$\hat{f} \in \mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{LC}}$.

% \paragraph{Base class: 2D log-concave densities.}
% A density $f: \mathbb{R}^{2} \to \mathbb{R}_{\ge 0}$ is
% \emph{log-concave} if $\log f$ is concave (with the convention
% $\log 0 = -\infty$). This is a nonparametric shape-constrained class
% that contains all Gaussians, exponentials, uniform distributions on
% convex sets, and any density with concave log; every log-concave
% density is unimodal.
% For any set of samples $y_1, \dots, y_M \in \mathbb{R}^{2}$ in general
% position, the (nonparametric) log-concave MLE
% \begin{equation}
%   \hat{f}_{\mathrm{LC}}
%   \;=\;
%   \arg\!\max_{f \in \mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{LC}}}
%   \;\;\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M} \log f(y_m)
%   \label{eq:lc-mle}
% \end{equation}
% exists uniquely and is piecewise log-linear on a triangulation of the
% sample support \citep{cule2010maximum,samworth2018recent}.


\paragraph{Mixture MALC ($K$ components).}
Because $\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{LC}}$ is unimodal, a single MALC fit
cannot represent the multimodal joints that arise when the posterior
concentrates on multiple compatible SCMs
(Section~\ref{sec:mixture-scm}). We therefore consider the mixture
family
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{MALC}}^{(K)}
  \;=\;
  \Big\{\,
    f \;=\; \sum_{k=1}^{K} \pi_k\, f_k
    \;:\;
    \pi \in \Delta^{K-1},\;\;
    f_k \in \mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{LC}}\;\;\forall k
  \,\Big\},
  \label{eq:malc-family}
\end{equation}
where each $f_k$ is a single-component MALC estimator obtained by the
procedure above. Membership in $\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{MALC}}^{(K)}$
permits up to $K$ distinct modes while retaining the shape constraint
within each component.

\paragraph{Mixture EM.}
For a fixed $K > 1$, components are combined via expectation--maxi\-mi\-sa\-tion
on the binned data. Let $\gamma_{j_0 j_1, k}^{(t)}$ denote the
responsibility of component $k$ for bin $(j_0, j_1)$ at iteration $t$,
and let $F_k^{(t)}(j_0, j_1) \coloneqq \int_{B_{j_0, j_1}} f_k^{(t)}$
denote the bin-integrated component density. The two updates are
\begin{align}
  \gamma_{j_0 j_1, k}^{(t+1)}
  \;&=\;
  \frac{\pi_k^{(t)}\, F_k^{(t)}(j_0, j_1)}
       {\sum_{j} \pi_j^{(t)}\, F_j^{(t)}(j_0, j_1)},
  \label{eq:malc-estep}
  \\[2pt]
  \pi_k^{(t+1)}
  \;&=\;
  \sum_{j_0, j_1} p_{j_0, j_1}\, \gamma_{j_0 j_1, k}^{(t+1)},
  \label{eq:malc-pi-update}
  \\[2pt]
  f_k^{(t+1)}
  \;&=\;
  \text{single-component refit on }
  \{p_{j_0, j_1}\, \gamma_{j_0 j_1, k}^{(t+1)}\}.
  \label{eq:malc-mstep}
\end{align}
Initialisation is a Voronoi assignment around modal peaks detected on
$p$ (with a deterministic augmentation when $K$ exceeds the number of
detected peaks); the iterations stop after five consecutive non-improving
log-likelihoods.

\paragraph{Algorithmic summary.}
Algorithm~\ref{alg:malc-mixture} collects the mixture EM iterations
described above and the BIC-based order selection described below into
a single pipeline.

\paragraph{Output.}
Once $K^{\star}$ is chosen, the final estimator is
\begin{equation}
  \hat{p}_{\mathrm{MALC}}(y_0, y_1)
  \;=\;
  \sum_{k=1}^{K^{\star}} \hat{\pi}_k^{\star}\, \hat{f}_k^{\star}(y_0, y_1),
  \label{eq:malc-output}
\end{equation}
a smooth density on the convex hull of the synthetic points, evaluated
at any query point $(y_0, y_1) \in \mathbb{R}^{2}$. Point estimates
follow directly:
$\hat{\mu}_{\mathrm{mean}} = \int (y_1 - y_0) \,d\hat{p}_{\mathrm{MALC}}$
and the 1D projection onto the diagonal $\tau = y_1 - y_0$ produces
the treatment-effect distribution used for OT aggregation in
Section~\ref{sec:ot-barycenter}. As $n \to \infty$, the absolute difference between
$\hat{\mu}_{\mathrm{mean}}$ and the true mean is bounded by $\delta$.
Since we set $K = 100$, this difference is below $0.02$ as n increases to infinity.

Theoretical guarantees for the
single-component MALC estimator (from which each $\hat{f}_k^\star$ is
drawn) are established in \citet{danisman2026malc}; here we treat the
mixture as a modelling device and do not claim additional properties for $\hat{p}_{\mathrm{MALC}}$ beyond those inherited componentwise.

\subsection{Population aggregation via the 2-Wasserstein barycenter}
\label{sec:ot-barycenter}
To turn per-query treatment-effect distributions into a single
population-level estimate we use the 2-Wasserstein barycenter.
Let $\hat{p}_i(\tau)$ denote the MALC-smoothed per-query
treatment-effect density (Section~\ref{sec:malc-multimodal}) for
query $X_i$ with cumulative distribution function $F_i$ and quantile
function $Q_i \coloneqq F_i^{-1}$. Given nonnegative weights
$\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_N$ summing to $1$, the (weighted)
2-Wasserstein barycenter is
\begin{equation}
  p^{\star}
  \;=\;
  \arg\!\min_{q}
  \sum_{i=1}^{N} \alpha_i\, W_2^{2}(\hat{p}_i, q),
  \qquad
  W_2^{2}(f, g)
  \;=\;
  \int_{0}^{1}\!\!\bigl(F_f^{-1}(t) - F_g^{-1}(t)\bigr)^{2}\,dt,
  \label{eq:barycenter-def}
\end{equation}
where $W_2$ is the 1D 2-Wasserstein distance in its quantile form.
In one dimension this problem admits a closed-form solution: for
measures on $\mathbb{R}$ with finite second moments, the barycenter
\eqref{eq:barycenter-def} exists uniquely and its quantile function is
the weighted average of the input quantile functions
\citep{aguehcarlier2011barycenters},
\begin{equation}
  Q^{\star}(t)
  \;=\;
  \sum_{i=1}^{N} \alpha_i\, Q_i(t),
  \qquad
  t \in (0, 1).
  \label{eq:agueh-carlier}
\end{equation}
\paragraph{Implementation.}
Because $Q^{\star}$ is defined explicitly by \eqref{eq:agueh-carlier},
no iterative optimisation is needed. Given the per-query densities on a
common grid, Algorithm~\ref{alg:ot-barycenter} computes the barycenter
in three quantile passes and one density-recovery pass. All steps are
$\mathcal{O}(N \!\cdot\! M)$ vector arithmetic; the barycenter is thus
much cheaper than the per-query MALC fits it aggregates.


\paragraph{Illustration on IHDP.}
Figure~\ref{fig:ihdp-ot} shows the aggregation on IHDP realisation $r{=}0$
with $N{=}200$ context observations and $N{=}10$ test queries. The ten
faded per-query densities peak around the individual $\tau$ values that
the model has inferred for each unit; the barycenter (solid green)
inherits their shape while collapsing to a single population summary.
The barycenter mode $\widehat{\mathrm{ATE}}_{\mathrm{OT\text{-}mode}} = 0.58$
is meaningfully closer to the true population ATE $0.63$ than either the
naive mean-of-means $0.47$. Mean-based
estimator underestimates because the per-query densities are right-skewed with heavier right shoulders than left, whereas the barycenter mode is invariant to this skew.

\paragraph{OT-mode and OT-mean point estimates.}
From $p^{\star}$ we extract two point estimates of the population
average treatment effect $\mathrm{ATE} = \mathbb{E}_{X}[\tau(X)]$:
\begin{equation}
  \widehat{\mathrm{ATE}}_{\mathrm{OT\text{-}mode}}
  \;=\; \arg\!\max_{\tau} p^{\star}(\tau),
  \qquad
  \widehat{\mathrm{ATE}}_{\mathrm{OT\text{-}mean}}
  \;=\; \int \tau\, p^{\star}(\tau)\, d\tau.
  \label{eq:ot-point-estimates}
\end{equation}
Both are functionals of $p^{\star}$ and hence deterministic given the
per-query densities. Here purpose is more to estimate the density rather than improving the point-wise estimation, but due to nature of skewness in the benchmark datasets, it also improves the results.



\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Mixture MALC from binned probabilities $\{p_{j_0, j_1}\}$.}
\label{alg:malc-mixture}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Binned joint $\{p_{j_0, j_1}\}$ on a $K_g \times K_g$ grid;
maximum order $K_{\max}$; synthetic-sample size $B$; tolerance $\Pi$.
\Ensure Multimodal density estimate
$\hat{p}_{\mathrm{MALC}} = \sum_{k} \hat\pi_k^\star \hat f_k^\star$.
\Function{FitComponent}{$w_{j_0, j_1}$}\Comment{single MALC on a weighted grid}
    % \State $\hat\mu_\ell \gets$ truncated-normal fixed-point mean per axis
    % \State $\alpha, \beta \gets$ Beta-jitter parameters calibrated so
    %     $\mathbb{E}[\text{Beta}] = \hat\mu_\ell$
    % \State draw $B$ bin indices $\{(j_0^b, j_1^b)\}_{b=1}^{B}$ from $w$
    % \State $y_b \gets \text{bin midpoint of }(j_0^b, j_1^b) + \text{jitter}(\alpha, \beta)$
    \State \Return log-concave MLE \eqref{eq:lc-mle} on $\{y_b\}$
\EndFunction
\For{$K \in \{1, \dots, K_{\max}\}$}
    \State detect modal peaks of $p$; assign each bin to nearest peak via Voronoi \Comment{init}
    \ForEach{$k \in \{1, \dots, K\}$}
        \State $f_k^{(0)} \gets \Call{FitComponent}{p \cdot \mathbf{1}\{\text{bin} \in \text{Voronoi cell } k\}}$
        \State $\pi_k^{(0)} \gets $ prior mass in Voronoi cell $k$
    \EndFor
    \State $t \gets 0$; $\ell^{\text{best}} \gets -\infty$; $\text{noimp} \gets 0$
    \While{$\text{noimp} < \Pi$}
        \State $F_k^{(t)}(j_0, j_1) \gets \int_{B_{j_0, j_1}} f_k^{(t)}$
        \State $\gamma_{j_0 j_1, k}^{(t+1)} \gets
            \pi_k^{(t)} F_k^{(t)}(j_0, j_1) / \sum_l \pi_l^{(t)} F_l^{(t)}(j_0, j_1)$
        \State $\pi_k^{(t+1)} \gets \sum_{j_0, j_1} p_{j_0, j_1}\, \gamma_{j_0 j_1, k}^{(t+1)}$
        \State $f_k^{(t+1)} \gets \Call{FitComponent}{p \cdot \gamma_{\cdot, k}^{(t+1)}}$
        \State $\ell^{(t+1)} \gets \sum_{j_0, j_1} p_{j_0, j_1}\,
                \log \sum_k \pi_k^{(t+1)} F_k^{(t+1)}(j_0, j_1)$
        \If{$\ell^{(t+1)} > \ell^{\text{best}}$}
            \State $\ell^{\text{best}} \gets \ell^{(t+1)}$; $\text{noimp} \gets 0$
        \Else
            \State $\text{noimp} \gets \text{noimp} + 1$
        \EndIf
        \State $t \gets t + 1$
    \EndWhile
    \State $\hat f^{(K)} \gets \sum_k \pi_k^{(t)} f_k^{(t)}$
    \State $\mathrm{BIC}(K) \gets
        -2 n_{\mathrm{eff}} \sum_{j_0, j_1} p_{j_0, j_1} \log \hat f^{(K)}(j_0, j_1)
        + (3K - 1)\log n_{\mathrm{eff}}$
        \Comment{Eq.~\eqref{eq:bic}}
\EndFor
\State $K^\star \gets \arg\!\min_K \mathrm{BIC}(K)$
\State \Return $\hat p_{\mathrm{MALC}} \gets \hat f^{(K^\star)}$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}


\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Weighted 2-Wasserstein barycenter of $N$ densities on a common
grid.}
\label{alg:ot-barycenter}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Per-query densities
$\{\hat{p}_i\}_{i=1}^{N}$ evaluated on grid
$x_1 < \cdots < x_M$ with spacing $\Delta x$; weights
$\alpha \in \Delta^{N-1}$ (default uniform); number of quantile levels
$M_\tau$ (default $4001$).
\Ensure Barycenter density $p^\star$ on the same grid.
\State $\tau_j \gets (j - \tfrac{1}{2}) / M_\tau$ \Comment{$j = 1,\dots,M_\tau$}
\ForEach{$i \in \{1, \dots, N\}$}
    \State $F_i(x_k) \gets \sum_{\ell \le k} \tfrac{1}{2}\bigl(\hat{p}_i(x_{\ell-1}) + \hat{p}_i(x_\ell)\bigr) \Delta x$
    \Comment{trapezoidal CDF, $F_i(x_0) = 0$}
    \State $F_i \gets F_i / F_i(x_M)$
    \State $Q_i(\tau_j) \gets \operatorname{interp}\bigl(\tau_j;\; F_i,\; x\bigr)$
    \Comment{invert CDF at each probability level}
\EndFor
\State $Q^\star(\tau_j) \gets \sum_{i=1}^{N} \alpha_i\, Q_i(\tau_j)$
    \Comment{Agueh--Carlier closed form \eqref{eq:agueh-carlier}}
\State $F^\star(x_k) \gets \operatorname{interp}\bigl(x_k;\; Q^\star,\; \tau\bigr)$
    \Comment{invert $Q^\star$ back to a CDF on the $x$-grid}
\State $p^\star(x_k) \gets \bigl(F^\star(x_{k+1}) - F^\star(x_{k-1})\bigr) / (2 \Delta x)$
    \Comment{central-difference derivative}
\State $p^\star \gets p^\star / \bigl(\Delta x\, \sum_k p^\star(x_k)\bigr)$
    \Comment{renormalise to integrate to 1}
\State \Return $p^\star$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}
